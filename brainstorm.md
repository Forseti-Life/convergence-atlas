# Brainstorm: Institutional Agents

**Started:** October 6, 2026  
**Purpose:** Preserve the conceptual model developed in dialogue while separating stipulated rules from claims requiring evidence.

## Central premise

Institutions can be treated as composite agents. Each contains people, departments, procedures, assets, and institutional memory, but presents an externally recognizable identity and pattern of action. Over time, increasingly capable artificial agents may make this analogy more literal by coordinating an institution's internal functions and representing it to outsiders.

An institution is not assumed to possess a perfectly unified mind. Its effective behavior emerges from its controlling coalition, subordinate agents, inherited rules, and environmental constraints.

## Working vocabulary

### Agent

An entity modeled as having identity, objectives, memory, resources, decision processes, and the capacity to act. A person, department, institution, government, or international body can be analyzed at its own level as an agent.

### Institution

A durable organization or organized system pursuing one or more interests. Businesses, religious organizations, government agencies, political parties, and civic organizations qualify. Ethnic identities and beliefs are not themselves institutions unless embodied or represented by organizations.

### Family

The controlling coalition of an institution: people bound by sufficiently durable alignment, loyalty, incentives, ideology, patronage, or kinship to exercise substantial control. Blood relationship is optional. Members can compete internally while remaining aligned against outside interests.

### Governmental family

The coalition that controls the state's principal decision-making, financial, administrative, and coercive institutions.

### Dynasty, ruling class, and controlling core

These must remain distinct:

1. **Dynasty or extended family:** everyone connected through descent, marriage, or recognized affiliation.
2. **Ruling class:** members receiving status, wealth, access, or institutional positions from the system.
3. **Controlling core:** the smaller coalition with effective decision rights and command of critical resources.

### Oligarchy

For this exercise, a government in which effective control remains concentrated in a relatively small governing family. The family may be dynastic, economic, political, military, bureaucratic, or ideological. This usage is broader than monarchy.

## Current model assumptions

### 1. Nested agency

The model is hierarchical:

> people → families → institutions → national governments → international alignments

Every level can be analyzed as an agent while also being a component of a larger agent.

### 2. Institutional interests

An institution advances a distinctive interest, constituency, mission, or identity. It also develops secondary objectives—survival, legitimacy, growth, resource acquisition, and preservation of leadership—that may compete with its stated purpose.

### 3. Controlling coalition

The family determines or negotiates the institution's effective objectives. Formal leaders, boards, owners, senior administrators, financiers, patrons, and influential constituencies may all belong to it. Control is substantial rather than absolute.

### 4. Sub-agents

Departments and subordinate institutions are semi-autonomous agents with local information and incentives. For the simplified national model, their actions are attributed to the country because they operate within its system. This does not necessarily mean personal loyalty to, or direct command by, the government.

### 5. Sovereign hierarchy

The national government is treated as the country's top-level institutional agent. The governmental family is its controlling coalition. This deliberately suppresses some complexity involving private autonomy, domestic opposition, multinational interests, and cross-border identities.

### 6. Preservation of control

Once established, a governing family treats retention of control as a primary objective. Leaders normally surrender control only when compelled by stronger incentives or forces: defeat, internal fracture, succession failure, mass opposition, institutional constraint, or a negotiated guarantee.

Formal changes in officeholders do not necessarily imply replacement of the underlying controlling coalition. Rival families seek to influence, enter, capture, replace, or fragment it.

### 7. Institutional conflict and inheritance

Competition can lead to coexistence, imitation, merger, capture, fragmentation, displacement, or elimination. When one institution defeats or absorbs another, some resources, knowledge, practices, symbols, and constituencies may pass to the winner. Transfer is incomplete: tacit knowledge may be lost, teachings may be rejected, and incompatible practices may be destroyed.

A winner's influence can increase without its coherence or effectiveness increasing. Expansion can also create overextension, legitimacy loss, and coordination costs.

### 8. International polarization

Under sufficiently severe interstate conflict, international institutions face pressure to align, split, lose members, become paralyzed, or be replaced by newly aligned bodies. Binary alignment is a simplifying limit case, not an assertion that neutrality and mediation never occur.

### 9. Internal alignment is imperfect

Large institutions do not eliminate principal-agent problems. Sub-agents can pursue local objectives, withhold information, compete bureaucratically, resist implementation, or redirect institutional resources. Open rebellion is not required for institutional goals to be subverted.

### 10. Generational growth and concentration

Families expand through descendants, marriage, alliance, and recruitment. This creates pressure toward fragmentation, but demographic growth does not automatically distribute political power. Succession rules, appointments, selective inheritance, control of security organizations, and exclusion of rival branches can reconcentrate authority in a small core.

## Illustrative case: Saudi Arabia

Saudi Arabia is a useful comparative case, although it is formally an absolute monarchy rather than an oligarchy. Since the modern kingdom's establishment in 1932, the House of Saud has expanded to many thousands of members; commonly repeated estimates are around 15,000, but exact counts are not public. Effective power belongs to a much smaller subset.

The case illustrates a transition from succession among the founder Ibn Saud's sons toward the grandchildren's generation, alongside concentration around King Salman's branch and Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. It therefore supports a preliminary distinction between rapid expansion of kinship and possible reconcentration of control.

**Research warning:** Family-size estimates, the number exercising meaningful power, and the current distribution of authority require reliable citations before publication. The case should not be used to infer a universal dilution rate.

## Preliminary mechanisms to examine

- **Aggregation:** How individual preferences become an institutional objective.
- **Alignment:** How a family maintains sufficient agreement to act collectively.
- **Selection:** Who enters the ruling class and controlling core.
- **Succession:** How authority crosses generations without splitting the institution.
- **Inheritance:** Which knowledge and practices survive institutional conflict.
- **Capture:** How a subordinate or outside family gains control of an institution.
- **Legitimacy:** Why people outside the family continue to cooperate.
- **Coercion:** How control of enforcement changes bargaining power.
- **Scale:** When growth increases influence and when it causes fragmentation.
- **Artificial coordination:** Whether AI agents make institutions behave more coherently as top-level agents.

## Open questions

1. What makes institutional agency more than a metaphor?
2. Is the institution's effective objective best modeled as the family's interest, an equilibrium among sub-agents, or an emergent objective of the organization itself?
3. What proportion of the population must cooperate for an oligarchy to remain stable?
4. Does artificial-agent coordination strengthen the controlling family or make institutional behavior more transparent and contestable?
5. Under what conditions does acquired knowledge survive a merger or conquest?
6. How should influence, coherence, legitimacy, and control be measured separately?
7. What determines whether generational expansion fragments power or produces a larger patronage network around a small core?
8. When does an international institution align, divide, remain neutral, or become irrelevant?
9. Which historical cases provide comparable evidence across three or more generations?
10. Is the eventual top-level agent the government, a bloc of governments, a transnational institution, or an adaptive network with no permanent center?

## Guardrails for the paper

- Present the framework as a thought experiment before testing it empirically.
- Do not equate ethnic or religious communities with the organizations claiming to represent them.
- Do not assume that institutional leaders faithfully represent all members or beneficiaries.
- Do not infer secret coordination merely from aligned outcomes.
- Distinguish formal authority from effective control.
- Distinguish demographic family growth from diffusion of decision rights.
- Label contemporary claims and estimates, then cite them before publication.

## Mandatory Apex observability assumption

Apex Institutions do not tolerate material concealment of data, core applications, infrastructure, dependencies, logs, model behavior, or recovery mechanisms by subordinate agents. Effective alignment requires registered and auditable visibility through Apex-authorized governance.

This does not eliminate privacy, encryption, least privilege, classified compartments, or separation of duties. A resource may be hidden from peers or the public but not from the authorized Apex oversight path. Detected material concealment triggers remediation with probability 1 under the model. Detection itself remains imperfect and should later be modeled separately.
