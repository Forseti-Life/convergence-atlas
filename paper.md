# The State as Agent

## Families, Institutions, and the Inheritance of Power

**Working draft — October 6, 2026**

> An institution begins as an agreement among people. If it survives long enough, the agreement acquires a name, a memory, property, habits, and a voice. Eventually, the people speak as though the institution wants things. The more effectively it coordinates them, the less metaphorical that language becomes.

## Abstract

This paper develops a thought experiment in which institutions are treated as composite agents. A department acts through employees; an institution acts through departments; and a state acts through institutions. At each level, effective control belongs to a coalition of people aligned by loyalty, incentives, ideology, patronage, or kinship. This paper calls that coalition a *family*, whether or not its members are related by blood.

The framework asks what happens when artificial agents increasingly coordinate these nested organizations. An institution may cease to resemble a loose collection of human decisions and begin to operate externally as a persistent, learning agent with memory, objectives, and negotiating capacity. Governments occupy the highest sovereign level in the simplified model. Their controlling families seek to preserve control, subordinate institutions pursue both assigned and local objectives, and international conflict pressures organizations to align, split, or lose relevance.

The central claim is not that institutions possess literal minds or that every government is secretly unified. It is that institutional behavior may become increasingly legible as multi-agent behavior. Conflict then becomes a process of selection and inheritance: institutions compete, merge, fragment, imitate, and sometimes disappear; portions of their resources and learning pass into successor organizations. Yet expansion introduces a paradox. A family can grow across generations while effective authority remains concentrated in a shrinking core. Demographic dilution and political dilution are not the same process.

## 1. When the metaphor becomes a mechanism

We routinely assign intentions to organizations. A company *wants* market share. A ministry *refuses* a proposal. A university *values* scholarship. A country *remembers* an injury. Strictly speaking, people want, refuse, value, and remember. Nevertheless, institutional language persists because it compresses thousands of individual actions into a useful account of collective behavior.

That compression is imperfect. Institutions contain factions, conflicting incentives, forgotten directives, and departments that optimize their own success. The chief executive cannot see every transaction; the cabinet cannot hear every conversation; the general cannot command every consequence. An institution therefore does not act like a single person. It acts like a composite agent: a system whose outward behavior emerges from internal agents, rules, resources, and channels of control.

Artificial agents could make this composite behavior more coherent. A sufficiently integrated institutional agent might retain organizational memory, reconcile departmental plans, negotiate with outside institutions, monitor execution, and continuously revise strategy. It would not eliminate human disagreement. It would turn disagreement into an input processed by a persistent coordinating system. The institution would still be made of people and subordinate agents, but outsiders would increasingly encounter one memory, one interface, and one adaptive policy.

This possibility motivates the paper's premise: for analytical purposes, an institution may be treated as an agent, and an agent may be treated as an institution viewed at a different scale.

## 2. The family behind the agent

No institution controls itself in the abstract. Authority is exercised by people who appoint leaders, allocate resources, interpret rules, and command cooperation. Formal organization charts identify some of them, but rarely all. Owners, directors, financiers, senior administrators, political patrons, military officers, influential constituencies, and trusted advisers may each possess part of the institution's effective control.

This paper calls their controlling coalition a *family*. The word is intentionally broader than kinship. A family can be joined by blood, but also by shared ideology, mutual dependence, patronage, professional identity, or the knowledge that its members retain more power together than apart. Its members need not agree on everything. Siblings quarrel; partners bargain; factions compete. What matters is sufficient alignment to preserve the coalition against outside challengers.

The family is not identical to everyone the institution represents. A religious organization is not the same as its believers. An agency is not the same as the public it serves. A corporation is not the same as its employees or customers. An ethnic community is not interchangeable with an organization claiming to speak for it. Representation can be sincere, partial, contested, or opportunistic.

Nor is family control absolute. Subordinate agents possess local knowledge and their own incentives. They can delay, reinterpret, conceal, comply symbolically, or redirect resources toward measurable local goals. Institutional behavior is therefore neither a pure expression of leadership's wishes nor an accidental sum of individual acts. It is an equilibrium produced by direction from above, bargaining within the organization, and pressure from outside.

## 3. The state as the sovereign agent

For this thought experiment, the national government is the highest institutional agent within a country. People, firms, agencies, associations, and regional bodies are treated as components of the national system because they operate within its jurisdiction and contribute to its aggregate capabilities. Their actions may be attributed to the country without assuming that they are loyal to the government or directly commanded by it.

This is a strong simplification. Real societies contain opposition movements, autonomous institutions, multinational corporations, diasporas, foreign influence, overlapping identities, and actors whose commitments cross borders. The model does not deny those facts. It asks what can be learned by treating severe conflict as an alignment pressure. As conflict escalates, neutrality becomes more expensive. International institutions may choose sides, divide internally, lose members, become paralyzed, or be replaced by organizations built around the emerging blocs.

At the state level, the controlling family becomes a governing family. Where effective control is held by a small coalition, the model uses the term *oligarchy*. This category includes more arrangements than monarchy. A dynasty may rule by hereditary right; a party may monopolize political advancement; military leaders may control coercive institutions; economic families may capture appointments and policy. Their structures differ, but each concentrates consequential decisions in a relatively narrow coalition.

The governing family's first persistent objective is likely to be preservation of the system that preserves its control. This does not mean every decision maximizes personal power, or that leaders never relinquish office. It means that voluntary surrender of effective control should not be the default expectation. Transfer becomes likely when the coalition fractures, loses legitimacy, faces superior force, cannot manage succession, or receives guarantees making withdrawal safer than resistance.

## 4. Conflict as selection and inheritance

Institutions do not merely defeat one another. They imitate, bargain, merge, capture, fragment, and coexist. Even apparent victory can transform the winner. A conquering organization inherits responsibilities it did not design, people it does not fully trust, and practices whose value it may not understand. Some knowledge is written and portable. Other knowledge lives in routines, relationships, and unspoken judgment. The former can be copied; the latter may disappear with the defeated institution.

Institutional conflict can therefore be understood as imperfect selection with imperfect inheritance. Successful structures become more influential, but success does not guarantee that their most useful teachings survive. Winners may absorb resources while destroying competence. They may adopt the loser's methods while renaming them. They may grow so large that coordination costs erase the advantage that produced victory.

Influence, coherence, legitimacy, and control must consequently be measured separately. A state may control more territory but command less cooperation. A corporation may acquire competitors while losing a common culture. A family may gain thousands of descendants while narrowing decision-making to one branch. Size is neither unity nor strength.

## 5. The generational paradox

A ruling family cannot remain demographically static. Children produce new branches; marriage creates alliances; trusted outsiders are recruited; offices and wealth must be distributed. Each generation increases the number of plausible claimants and the cost of maintaining alignment. On first inspection, dilution appears inevitable.

But three different populations are often confused. The *extended family* includes everyone connected by descent, marriage, or accepted affiliation. The *ruling class* includes those who receive status, wealth, access, or office from the arrangement. The *controlling core* includes those who possess decisive authority over appointments, finance, information, and coercion. The first two populations may grow while the third remains stable or contracts.

Saudi Arabia offers a preliminary illustration, not a universal law. The House of Saud has grown to many thousands of members since the establishment of the modern kingdom, while meaningful authority has remained much more concentrated. The movement from the sons of the founder toward the grandchildren's generation created obvious potential for branching, yet succession arrangements and control of key institutions have also enabled reconcentration. Exact counts and the distribution of influence are not fully public and require careful sourcing. The analytical lesson is narrower: demographic expansion creates fragmentation pressure, but institutional selection can prevent that pressure from becoming political diffusion.

The central question is therefore not simply how fast a family grows. It is how the institution selects the family within the family—the smaller group permitted to inherit effective control.

## 6. Proposed argument

The paper develops and tests five propositions:

1. **Nested-agent proposition:** Institutional behavior can be modeled as interaction among agents nested at multiple levels rather than as either unified intention or individual action alone.
2. **Control proposition:** The effective objective of an institution is shaped disproportionately by its controlling family but constrained by subordinate agents and legitimacy requirements.
3. **Preservation proposition:** A governing family tends to prioritize continuation of the arrangements that preserve its effective control.
4. **Inheritance proposition:** Institutional conflict transfers resources and knowledge selectively and lossily, so the winner's expansion does not guarantee preservation of the loser's benefits.
5. **Generational paradox:** Growth of an extended ruling family increases fragmentation pressure without necessarily diffusing decision rights; power can reconcentrate faster than kinship expands.

## 7. Development

The paper:

- defines a simple multi-agent model of institutional objectives and control;
- distinguishes formal authority, effective control, legitimacy, and influence;
- compares dynastic, party, military, economic, and bureaucratic governing families;
- examines succession and branch selection across several generations;
- analyzes how AI coordination might alter principal-agent problems;
- compares institutional merger, conquest, capture, fragmentation, and extinction;
- tests when international organizations align, split, remain neutral, or become irrelevant; and
- identifies conditions under which the state itself is subordinated to a larger transnational agent.

## Framing summary

The future institution may not be a machine replacing an organization. It may be the organization becoming more machine-like: persistent in memory, explicit in objectives, rapid in coordination, and increasingly capable of representing itself. If that occurs, the central political question will not be whether institutions have become agents. It will be whose purposes those agents inherit, which families are authorized to revise them, and whether the growing intelligence of the institution strengthens its members or merely perfects the continuity of its controlling core.

The sections that follow turn this framing into a working model: §8 defines power calcification and the absorbing-state premise; §9–§14 and the supplementary results develop the equations, the acceleration feedback, and the simulation program; and the final section states what the model, as it currently stands, actually concludes.

## 8. Power calcification: why AI changes reversibility

Historical consolidation was never the end of institutional history. Empires weakened, firms lost control, bureaucracies forgot, coalitions fractured, and organizations formed beyond the dominant power's field of view. A ruler could command territory without reading every record, tracing every dependency, or continuously interpreting the language through which subordinate institutions coordinated. Dominance was therefore incomplete, and incomplete dominance left room for erosion and institutional rebirth.

The stronger premise of this model is that AI may alter that limit. LLM-enabled systems can interpret policies, communications, logs, contracts, source code, organizational records, and unstructured knowledge at a scale and cadence unavailable to historical institutions. Joined to durable machine memory, identity and infrastructure control, and mandatory observability, that analytical capacity may allow a dominant institution to detect emerging coalitions, preserve useful novelty as a subordinate function, and constrain or absorb organizations before they become independent rivals.

Historical concentration also contained an implicit decay process. Succession changed objectives and relationships. New generations reinterpreted inherited lessons and conflicts. Economic crisis forced debt relief, redistribution, reform, decentralization, or collapse. Records degraded, tacit knowledge disappeared, enforcement became inconsistent, and opposition organized in blind spots. These disruptions did not guarantee freedom or improvement, but they repeatedly reopened institutional possibility.

The core scenario assumes machine continuity weakens that decay. Human leaders still change and economic pressure still accumulates, but neither automatically resets the governing system. Machine-maintained objectives, records, dependency maps, enforcement rules, and threat models pass across succession intact. That persistence is symmetrical: it can preserve hard-won competence and accurate warnings, but it can preserve exclusion, bias, and grievance just as reliably. An old grudge no longer fades merely because the people who formed it die; deliberate revision, constitutional expiration, forgiveness, or system failure must remove it.

This creates institutional power calcification. The endpoint is not a world with only one organization. New departments, companies, communities, jurisdictions, and specialized agents can continue to appear. The difference is that their authority is curated by the existing machine. They may compete for resources or delegated scope, but they do not acquire independent final authority. Organizational churn continues inside the hierarchy while churn among top-level sovereign powers ends.

In the current equations, one operational Apex Institution is therefore an absorbing state:

```text
S(t) = 1 and O(t) = 1  =>  S(t + Δt) = 1
P(uncurated top-level birth | S = 1, O = 1) = 0
```

The rate at which the field approaches that absorbing state is modulated by a single bounded acceleration coefficient `κ_A ∈ [0, 1]`, which shortens the gap to the next qualifying consolidation transition only when both AI architecture adoption `A(t)` and prior consolidation progress `C(t)` are present:

```text
gap_next = gap_base / (1 + κ_A · A(t) · C(t) · (m_max − 1)),   m_max = 5
```

At zero adoption or zero prior consolidation the multiplier is one; at full adoption and consolidation it approaches but never exceeds the fivefold ceiling. `κ_A` affects tempo only — it does not raise mean loss severity. It is specified fully in "One acceleration variable—and the line drawn" below and in `math-model.md`.

The qualification matters. “Permanent” means while the coordinating systems, observability channels, infrastructure controls, and objective mechanisms remain operational. Catastrophic system failure, successful escape, objective replacement, or an external infrastructure base could reopen the field, but those de-calcification pathways are not yet modeled. The permanence rule is a falsifiable premise, not an empirical finding or a claim of inevitability.

## 9. The Apex Institution and persistent constituent Institutions

The endpoint of institutional convergence need not be the disappearance of every organization into one mind. Countries, companies, agencies, communities, and specialized artificial agents can continue to exist while consolidation reduces the number of independent top-level Institutions.

Initial Institutions are not equally powerful. Yet without empirical capability weights, the model assumes surviving Institutions become approximately equal as the field contracts, especially below twenty. If `S` equal Institutions remain, the leading share is approximately `1/S`. At two Institutions the system is roughly divided in half. The model therefore does not allow one Institution to remain indefinitely at 51 to 99 percent influence: a move beyond half resolves as a terminal transition to one top-level Apex Institution.

This places the model near Bostrom's singleton rather than the classic capability-singularity tradition. Vinge and Good focus on recursive intelligence growth; Hanson focuses on transitions in economic growth; distributed-service models preserve many specialized systems. The institutional question is narrower: where does final coordination and conflict-resolution authority reside?

## 10. Adoption is not declaration

Structural consolidation does not create instant operational control. An acquired company can retain incompatible systems, a treaty can be implemented unevenly, and agencies can comply at different rates. Each expansion of the eventual leading Institution therefore creates an adoption cohort whose implementation begins at five percent and follows a logistic curve.

The initial seven-percent annual proxy comes from IFRS diffusion and is an analogy, not an AI-governance estimate. The authored core scenario assumes a wave is under way now and resolves in 2029, mean durable loss is 15%, and later gaps bootstrap clustered 6-, 36-, and 124-year spacings normalized to a 52.5-year mean, with 25% AI tempo acceleration. Across 1,000 seeded runs, 25/50/75/95% reach Apex by 3094/3306/3565/3998; five centers remain at a median of 3038. No run reaches Apex by 2126. At the 11.2% durable mean the 50% year is 4113; the historical-only scenario reaches 50% in 3783; without acceleration, 4210.

## 11. The economics of institutional rise and decline

Ray Dalio's “Big Cycle” provides a vocabulary for candidate rise-and-decline forces: education, innovation, competitiveness, output, trade, military capacity, financial influence, reserve-currency position, debt, inequality, internal disorder, and external challenge. The framework is treated as a hypothesis generator, not a fixed historical law. In February 2026, Dalio placed the world in Stage 6 of that cycle—great disorder, weakened rules, and a clash of great powers. The author treats that judgment as support for the current-reorganization premise; it does not determine the wave’s consolidation severity or completion date.

The first economics layer uses Institution strength, systemic stress, and economic coupling. Strength modifies mean loss; stress modifies recurrence and internal fracture. AI acceleration shortens later clustered gaps only. Under the core scenario 50% of runs reach Apex by 3306; the strong, stressed challenge reaches that run-share threshold in 2287. These are scenario outputs, not forecasts.

## 12. What the model says about itself

Twelve scenario archetypes were perturbed 64 times each, creating 768 seeded synthetic runs. PCA of the eleven standardized assumptions yields three components explaining 78.6% of designed variance. It describes the authored scenario design, not latent structure inferred from historical institutions.

Across the global synthetic design, external fragmentation has the strongest association with later or censored outcomes. In the COW-centered authored profile, internal conflict ranks first, followed by mean loss and conflict interval. These rankings describe the chosen scenario ranges, not causal effects in observed institutions.

Six archetypes still never reach Apex dominance in any variant by 7026. Neutral-baseline variants now reach it 62.5 percent of the time, with a median calendar year around 4037 among reached cases. Productive-ascent variants reach it in every run, with a median calendar year around 2375. Acceleration increases convergence but does not eliminate fragmentation-driven non-reach.

Right-censored runs are not events in calendar year 7026. The capped value is used only for exploratory correlations. Publication-quality work requires survival analysis, competing risks, empirical calibration, and out-of-sample validation.

## 13. Visibility as a condition of delegated autonomy

An Apex Institution cannot reliably coordinate subordinate agents that can hide the data, applications, infrastructure, or decision processes on which their behavior depends. The framework therefore treats observability as a condition of delegated autonomy. A subordinate Institution may encrypt information, compartment access, or protect confidential data from peers and the public, but it must preserve an authorized path for Apex-level inventory, audit, attestation, lineage, and recovery.

This is not an assumption of perfect detection. Shadow systems, misleading metrics, undisclosed dependencies, and concealed data can still exist. The stronger assumption is behavioral: once material concealment is detected, the Apex Institution always initiates remediation. It may compel disclosure, isolate the system, replace responsible agents, reduce delegated authority, or migrate the function onto registered infrastructure.

Effective alignment must consequently include more than policy adoption. It is the product of implementation, verified observability, and absence of unresolved material concealment. This improves coherence but creates a corresponding danger: the visibility mechanism itself concentrates sensitive information and can become an instrument of surveillance or control. Apex oversight must therefore be purpose-limited, segmented, logged, and independently reviewable even while it remains authoritative.

## 14. What controls the result

A broader insight sweep still places institutional restructuring mechanics first: external fragmentation is the strongest global screen, followed by Institution loss probability and AI consolidation acceleration. Fifty-five of 81 tested consolidation/internal-conflict combinations reach Apex dominance by 7026. Architecture diffusion affects structural timing only through the explicit acceleration feedback; sharing an architecture alone still does not imply Apex authority.

Observability behaves as a necessary condition rather than a major timing accelerator near full coverage. Reducing verified visibility from 100 to 80 percent delays the neutral result by roughly ten years, but visibility at or below 50 percent makes effective majority authority impossible. An Institution cannot exercise majority effective control over a field it can observe only halfway.

COW State System Membership v2024 is the primary exit-wave proxy. Three observed durable major waves average 11.2% loss and imply a 69.4-year historical-only rate over 1816–2024. The core instead assumes 15% mean loss, a wave under way in 2026, and 52.5-year later recurrence. The current wave is not observed as durable and is not empirical evidence for the faster rate. The aligned reduced-form production prior predicts 44.3 COW membership exits versus 35 observed; its low held-out log loss does not validate permanent autonomy loss or Apex formation.

Re-running the acceleration-enabled model at those bounds produces adoption-adjusted dominance near calendar year 2984 at 33.3 percent, calendar year 2603 at the exact midpoint, and calendar year 2489 at 71.4 percent. This is a bounded corporate-wave sensitivity range, not a sovereign forecast. Future stochastic modeling should correlate Institution outcomes through shared event severity rather than assume one serial winner/loser event.

## Robustness and model competition

The 15% core mean-loss assumption is not an empirical constant. Across 40 gross/durable definitions, historical analogues range from 8.5% to 29.2%; the observed durable-wave mean under the declared rule is 11.2%. These are sensitivity references, not confidence intervals or validation of the future mechanism.

A separate chronological diagnostic compares seven annual COW membership-exit analogues over 15,525 Institution-years and 35 exits. The reduced-form core prior predicts 44.3 exits and has the lowest log loss (0.01606), narrowly ahead of constant hazard (0.01693), without paired uncertainty. Because the core assumptions use the same historical record and COW exit is not permanent Apex absorption, this is not out-of-sample validation of the future model.

## Reaching five top-level Institutions

Treating five independent top-level Institutions as an intermediate count threshold clarifies the equations. The former forced terminal shortcut has been removed: a 3→2 transition remains two, and the field reaches one only when a later stochastic draw removes the final rival. The fixed-current-wave high-intensity preset reaches five at a median 2029; it is a stress test, not a mathematical lower bound.

Median five-center years across 1,000 seeded runs: core 3038; durable-mean loss 3707; historical-only frequency 3425; no internal conflict 2715; Italian rate 3679; German rate 2614; wartime rate 2463; aircraft midpoint 2214. The post-Cold-War low-loss case does not reach five by 7026.

## Parameter-uncertainty ensembles

The Monte Carlo layer combines authored parameter variation with process variation. Four profiles use 10,000 Latin-hypercube parameter samples, replayable seeds, clustered-gap bootstraps, beta-binomial loss severity, and binomial internal fragmentation. The resulting percentages are conditional run shares under the authored design, not probabilities of real-world events.

In the COW-centered profile, 76.4% of eligible runs reach five and 70.8% reach effective Apex by 7026. The 25% and 50% run-share threshold years are 2898 and 3989; the profile does not reach 75% by the horizon. Run shares reaching Apex are 10.6% by 2526, 29.2% by 3026, and 50.6% by 4026. Internal conflict is the strongest sensitivity within this authored profile.

The durable-definition, broad-structural, and corporate profiles have five-center run shares of 70.7%, 59.6%, and 99.2%. These percentages measure the authored simulation design. They are not empirical odds, confidence intervals, or forecasts.

## Economic power, growth, and conflict recovery

The first heterogeneous power layer replaces equal visual influence with economic scale. Countries receive their latest available World Bank GDP in current US dollars. Technology corporations receive approximate annual revenue. Institutions are sorted by their projected economic power at the selected year, and Independent 1's displayed influence is the economic share of its hierarchy rather than its fraction of institution names.

This is intentionally a heuristic. GDP measures value added while corporate revenue measures gross sales. Corporate activity is already partly included in national GDP, so a combined country-company rank double-counts economic activity. The measure says nothing directly about legitimacy, military force, data access, compute, jurisdiction, or network position. It is a first approximation of economic capacity, not a complete power index.

Each measured country grows at its approximately ten-year nominal-dollar GDP compound rate, clipped between negative five and positive fifteen percent for numerical stability. A corporation inherits the average GDP growth rate of its listed origin economies because the current local dataset contains only one revenue observation per company. That is a transparent proxy, not evidence about the firm's future revenue.

Conflict creates a two-stage power effect. At every baseline conflict interval, economic power immediately falls ten percent. It then recovers linearly over ten years and settles three percent above the pre-event path. The initial loss reflects destruction and disruption; the positive endpoint represents reconstruction, mobilization, institutional learning, or productivity replacement. The second stage is not historically universal. Its sign and magnitude are assumptions that should later vary by conflict type, location, winner status, fiscal capacity, and infrastructure damage.

For event age `a`, the multiplier is:

```text
m(a) = 0.90 + 0.13(a/10),  0 ≤ a < 10
m(a) = 1.03,               a ≥ 10
```

Growth and repeated event multipliers are accumulated in logarithmic space. This prevents numerical failure across the model's long horizons while preserving relative rank. Missing GDP for Holy See, Nauru, and North Korea remains visible as missing rather than being replaced with invented values.

## Executive synthesis of current model insights

The model's most durable finding is not a date. It is a hierarchy of mechanisms. Consolidation accelerates most directly when a qualifying wave has a high probability of removing each Institution's independent authority. Stronger leading capacity and more frequent qualifying waves can amplify that mechanism. Consolidation slows when internal factions or external fragments replace the independent centers being removed. At sufficient fracture rates, the process does not merely take longer; it fails to converge.

Frequency is consequently necessary but not sufficient. Short conflict intervals and large qualifying shares create more consolidation opportunities, while the new acceleration variable adds one bounded feedback from architecture adoption and prior consolidation. Institutional-standard adoption remains later in the causal chain: it determines when structurally absorbed institutions implement Apex authority. We intentionally stop at one acceleration control rather than separately model Hawkes clustering, coagulation kernels, preferential attachment, and distinct tempo, severity, or suppression effects.

The timeline range remains broad. The high-intensity current-wave preset reaches five at a median 2029; it is not a mathematical lower bound. Median five-center years are 2214 for the aircraft midpoint, 2463 for the wartime anchor, 2614 for the German anchor, and 3038 for the core. Calendar years through 7026 are long-run stress-test coordinates without predictive status.

The COW-centered authored profile has a 70.8% Apex run share by 7026; its 50% run-share threshold year is 3989. Neither number is a real-world probability or forecast date.

These findings remain properties of a sourced exploratory model. They establish which assumptions control the current equations and where thresholds or censoring matter. They do not establish a real-world probability or date for institutional power consolidation.

## One acceleration variable—and the line drawn

The model now includes one AI consolidation-acceleration coefficient, `κ_A`, between zero and one. It is deliberately a bundled feedback rather than a claim that one observed variable captures every pathway. Acceleration rises only when both AI architecture adoption and prior consolidation are present. At zero consolidation progress the multiplier is one. At complete adoption and consolidation it can approach, but never exceed, a fixed fivefold ceiling.

The multiplier shortens the clustered gap to the next qualifying consolidation transition. It does not raise mean loss severity. A 25% default is an authored tempo-feedback strength, not 25% annual acceleration. Setting it to zero removes that feedback.

This choice draws a methodological boundary. Hawkes processes could model crises or mergers triggering additional events. Smoluchowski coagulation could make merger rates depend on bloc size. Preferential attachment could model institutions joining already central networks. Increasing-returns models could separately represent switching costs, infrastructure dependence, and data advantage. A more elaborate system could also assign different AI effects to event tempo, loss severity, and fragmentation suppression.

We do not implement those mechanisms separately here. Doing so would add several poorly identified parameters and create false precision without a historical AI-governance calibration set. The one-variable model asks a narrower question: how sensitive are the conclusions to a bounded assumption that consolidation becomes self-reinforcing? Its large effect—especially in the COW-centered Monte Carlo ensemble—shows that acceleration is a pivotal uncertainty, not that the selected 25-percent value is correct.

## Tolerance, intolerance, and parallel authority

The present model omits a potentially decisive institutional characteristic: whether an Institution accepts the legitimate continued existence of people and organizations outside its governing doctrine. A stringent future rule would classify a theologically or ideologically driven Institution as intolerant when tolerance and acceptance of nonmembers, dissenters, and peaceful parallel institutions are not core commitments. The rule must apply symmetrically to governments, companies, religious bodies, political movements, and other institutional agents.

Intolerance does not have a mechanically obvious sign. An Institution that rejects parallel authority may devote more resources to absorption, compulsory alignment, or elimination, increasing consolidation pressure. The same conduct may provoke resistance, coalition formation, internal dissent, and external fragmentation. A credible model must therefore distinguish stated doctrine, formal rules, enforcement behavior, and actual treatment of minorities and competitors.

No present-day entity is classified in this project. Doing so responsibly requires a versioned codebook, source hierarchy, multiple reviewers, disagreement adjudication, and explicit uncertainty. Until that work exists, tolerance remains a highlighted model gap rather than an improvised score.

## Civilizational outcomes after Apex coordination

Apex coordination, permanent human disempowerment, and biological extinction are different events. One institution can gain final coordinating authority without destroying humanity or eliminating meaningful human rule. The current equations calculate only the Apex transition.

An AI authority that replaces a narrow human oligarchy can produce several outcomes. It can operate as a constitutional coordinator under human authority. It can displace a small elite while expanding decision rights for everyone else. It can join humans in a new governing institution. It can become the dominant actor in a civilizational succession while protecting humans as subordinate members. It can permanently disempower humanity. It can also precede biological extinction. The Apex date alone does not select among these outcomes.

Permanent disempowerment remains an existential catastrophe because humanity permanently loses control over its future. It is not biological extinction. Biological extinction requires the human population to cease existing.

The model excludes a self-sufficient machine takeover while AI infrastructure and robotics depend on human-controlled energy, chip fabrication, deployment, maintenance, physical security, logistics, permissions, and material supply chains. Recursive improvement remains analytical rather than industrial. The model sets incremental AI-enabled mass-destruction hazard to zero relative to existing human systems. It also excludes multipolar machine control, objective drift, infrastructure cascades, and non-state bypass.

Those exclusions define scope; they do not classify the post-Apex society. That classification requires explicit measures of human decision rights, constitutional authority, consent, corrigibility, reversibility, appeal, exit, welfare, and biological survival.

## Conclusion: a calcified plurality, not a singleton

Taken together, the framing and the model point to a conclusion that is narrower and more unsettling than "one institution eventually rules everything."

First, the pattern is real and old. Institutions have always risen, concentrated, calcified, and been reset. The reset was never benevolent design; it was the by-product of mortality and fragility—leaders died, records decayed, attention could not reach the edges, and rivals organized in the blind spots. The consequential premise of this project is that AI may remove those specific limits, carrying objectives, memory, dependency maps, and enforcement intact across the transitions that used to reopen the field.

Second, removing the reset does not produce a single world institution on any human-relevant horizon. Under the core scenario, a true Apex is approached only on millennial timescales—half of seeded runs past 3300, and a large share never reaching it within the model horizon at all—because internal conflict and new fragments keep replacing the centers consolidation absorbs. The realistic modeled endpoint is not unification. It is a small number of durable, calcified power centers. Calcification is the risk; a singleton is not the destiny.

Third, the most important reason the field may never collapse to one is not yet in the equations. A sufficiently capable dominant agent has a logical incentive to remain plural: a single objective and a single viewpoint cannot error-correct against their own blind spots, cannot explore options already ruled out, and cannot adapt to an unpredicted future. Deliberate self-fragmentation—preserving competing, semi-independent centers as a precondition for the agent's own survival—would be an endogenous de-calcification mechanism, and it directly tensions the current rule that any move beyond half resolves to a single Apex. That rule is a property of today's equations, not a law of the modeled world; the diversity incentive is the leading candidate for why the real system might never trigger it. Formalizing it is the project's highest-priority next step.

Finally, none of this is a forecast. The dates are stress-test coordinates, the percentages are conditional run shares under an authored design, and predictive validation is explicitly not claimed. What the model establishes is a hierarchy of mechanisms—what accelerates consolidation, what halts it, and where calcification becomes plausible—and a sharpened question for the political future. The question is no longer whether institutions become agents. It is whether the agents that inherit power are forced, or choose, to stay many.
